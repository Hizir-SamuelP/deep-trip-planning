# Transit and Maps

## 1. Set the origin first

Once the lodging address is known, choose an origin: the nearest station and its lines for transit trips, the lodging plus parking conditions for road trips, and one origin per segment for multi-city trips. Write each day as “origin → first stop” and “last stop → origin.” If several days require detours, the lodging—not the route—is the problem. Use a leg table with date, outbound, return, one-way duration/cost, and notes. Mark monetary figures as estimates with a source; the route is the real value.

## 2. Five public-transit failures

### Express services skip the home station

Small stops often get local trains only. State which service class stops at the origin and write “take local service X; check the train display.” This prevents a common accidental overshoot.

### Same-name stations may be separate buildings

Operators can use the same name for stations that require a five- to ten-minute underground walk. Check cross-operator transfers and write the walking allowance, especially before an early train.

### A place name is not a location

Hotels and venues borrow famous-neighborhood names from several kilometers away. Route from the actual address and nearest station, not the listing name. Use the platform's distance figure only as a cross-check.

### Direct is not always fastest, and fastest is not always best

With large bags, a slower zero-transfer airport bus can beat three changes. To reach a particular platform, a route that arrives in the right station building may be safer than a nominally faster cross-station transfer. For a golden hour, an earlier slow service may arrive before a later express. Give two or three options and say when each one wins.

### Last services can be surprisingly early

Airport buses, sightseeing lines, and rural branches may finish at seven or eight in the evening. Check and write the last departure for every low-frequency dependency; an airport bus may be useless for a late flight.

## Road trips

Replace transit traps with actual map-checked drive times, parking availability and costs, fuel/charging points, seasonal road closures and equipment requirements, no-signal sections, and licensing recognition. Add 20–30% to each checked driving time and add separate city-parking time; distance alone is not a time estimate.

## 3. Passes: show the arithmetic

Default to pay-as-you-go: transit card, contactless card, or individual tickets. Buy a pass only for a day or segment where the calculation wins: “three rides A + B + C ≈ X; day pass Y; saving Z.” Multi-day passes often lose because walking days and light-use days are included. A tourism bureau's promoted pass is an average-traveler product, not proof for this route. State why a rejected pass is not being bought and how many uses would make it pay back.

Also check payment mechanics: whether a transit card works on a phone, whether foreign cards can top it up or tap directly, whether a physical card has a deposit, and where to buy it. Prepare the conservative path—cash for a physical card—until verified otherwise.

## 4. Choose the local map stack

Do not assume Google Maps works everywhere. In some countries the walking or transit layer is incomplete and local apps are the practical standard. Before writing transit, find the local visitor map/transfer app, language support, account requirements, and whether address search needs local script. Name this as an operating constraint, not a personal preference.

## 5. Three map layers

| Layer | Tool | Job | Hard limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Overview | A custom map, grouped by day | Plan the night before and judge route order | Not offline and not turn-by-turn navigation |
| Execution | Saved places in the main map app | Find venues, current crowding, and hours | Requires manually saving key points |
| Navigation | Transit/navigation function or local app | Execute each actual leg | Transit queries need internet; offline maps usually cover driving only |

Before departure: download regional offline maps; star only the ten to fifteen places whose failure ruins half a day; save screenshots of reserved venues' local-language names and addresses; and share the map link with companions. Each evening select only tomorrow's layer. Each morning check origin to first stop, including the exact service, transfer, exit, road, or parking lot. Do not improvise extra stops mid-route.

## 6. Write a leg a stranger can follow

“The subway is convenient” fails. “Take local X from the origin to Y, about 20 minutes with no transfer; express trains skip the origin, so read the display” works. The test for every leg is whether someone who does not know the city can execute it without researching again.
